One year, two profits

Cobalt Trail Packs made 10,000 backpacks and sold 8,000. Its profit was $70,000, or $110,000. Both are correct. Here's why, and how to see which parts of a company really earn money.

6.1

Two ways to cost a unit

AbsorptionVariableFixed overhead

The only difference: fixed overhead

  • Both methods count direct materials, direct labor and variable overhead: $30 a pack
  • Absorption costing also loads $200,000 ÷ 10,000 = $20 of fixed overhead into each pack: $50
  • Variable costing treats fixed manufacturing overhead as a period cost. Selling and admin costs are period costs under both

6.3

Why the profits differ

Deferred in inventoryReleased laterWhy managers care

$40,000 waits in the warehouse

  • Year 1: 8,000 of the 10,000 packs sell, taking $160,000 of fixed overhead with them. 2,000 × $20 = $40,000 stays in inventory, so absorption NOI is $40,000 higher: $110,000 vs $70,000
  • Year 2: 12,000 packs sell, including the 2,000 from inventory. The $40,000 is released, so absorption NOI is $40,000 lower: $210,000 vs $250,000

Over two years, it evens out

Profit from overproducing?

  • Variable costing profit only moves when sales move
  • Under absorption costing, making 16,000 packs that nobody bought lifts profit to $170,000
  • That's why managers prefer variable costing inside the company: it fits CVP and can't be pumped up by building inventory. Absorption costing is still required for outside reports and taxes

Segmented income statements

TraceableCommonSegment margin

Would it disappear?

  • A traceable fixed cost exists because of the segment: the website for Online, the store leases for Retail
  • A common fixed cost supports both segments, like the CEO's salary and headquarters
  • Drop Retail and its $120,000 of traceable costs disappear. The $100,000 of common costs stay

6.5

Segment break-even

Traceable ÷ CM ratioAdd the common costs

$300,000 + $300,000 ≠ $800,000

  • Segment break-even = traceable fixed ÷ segment CM ratio
  • Each channel covers its own traceable costs at $300,000 of sales
  • The company must also cover the $100,000 of common costs, at today's 50/50 mix (overall CM ratio 50%)

6.6

Common mistakes

Missing costsBad allocation basesSplitting common costs

Don't drop a profitable segment

  • Splitting common costs by floor space charges Retail $90,000, so it seems to lose money
  • But the common costs don't leave with Retail. Dropping it throws away $80,000 of segment margin
  • Also avoid leaving out costs from the value chain (design, delivery, customer service) and allocating with bases that don't drive the cost

Keep Retail, or drop it?

Retail's report card$80,000segment margin, nothing allocated
Company net operating income$100,000both channels open
Retail adds $80,000 to profit. Splitting the common costs changes how it looks, not what it earns.
