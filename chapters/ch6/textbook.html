Variable Costing and Segment Reporting

The same company can report two different profits for the same year, depending on how it treats fixed factory overhead. This chapter compares the two methods, then splits a company into segments to see which parts actually earn money. The example is Cobalt Trail Packs, a fictional backpack maker.

Section 6.1Two ways to cost a unit

Ground rules

To keep the comparison clean, this chapter uses actual costs (no predetermined overhead rate), spreads fixed manufacturing overhead over the units actually produced, and assumes the variable cost per unit and total fixed overhead stay the same from year to year.

Unit product cost

The only difference between the two methods is fixed manufacturing overhead:

  • Absorption costing (required for external reports) treats all manufacturing costs as product costs, fixed overhead included.
  • Variable costing treats only variable manufacturing costs as product costs. Under variable costing, fixed manufacturing overhead is a period cost, expensed in full each year.

Cobalt Trail sells packs for $80. Each pack uses $14 of direct materials, $10 of direct labor and $6 of variable overhead. Fixed manufacturing overhead is $200,000 a year, and the factory makes 10,000 packs a year.

Absorption: $30 + $200,000 ÷ 10,000 = $50. Selling and administrative costs ($5 a pack variable, $90,000 a year fixed) are period costs under both methods.

Exam tip

Variable selling expenses are never part of unit product cost. Only manufacturing costs can be.

Key terms
Contribution format
Separates variable and fixed costs; used with variable costing.
Traditional format
Sales − cost of goods sold = gross margin; used with absorption costing.

Section 6.3Why the profits differ

Reconciling the difference

Under absorption costing, each pack carries $20 of fixed overhead. The 2,000 unsold packs took 2,000 × $20 = $40,000 of Year 1's fixed overhead into inventory, so it isn't expensed yet. Variable costing expensed all $200,000. That is the whole difference: $110,000 − $70,000 = $40,000.

In Year 2 the factory again made 10,000 packs but sold 12,000, including the 2,000 left from Year 1. Those packs bring their $40,000 of fixed overhead out of inventory and into cost of goods sold:

Year 1 (made 10,000, sold 8,000)Year 2 (made 10,000, sold 12,000)

+ Fixed MOH deferred in (released from) inventory40,000(40,000)

When a company produces more than it sells, absorption costing reports the higher profit, because some fixed overhead waits in inventory. Over the two years together, both methods report $320,000: the timing differs, the total doesn't.

Exam tip

Difference in NOI = change in inventory units × fixed manufacturing overhead per unit.

Why managers like variable costing

  • It fits CVP. Variable costing keeps fixed costs fixed, so contribution margin and break-even work directly.
  • Profit follows sales. Under absorption costing, a manager can raise profit just by producing more than needed, piling fixed overhead into inventory. Variable costing profit only rises when sales rise.
  • It supports decisions. Absorption costing makes fixed overhead look like a per-unit cost that varies with volume, which can mislead pricing and keep-or-drop decisions.

Absorption costing is still required for external financial reports and income taxes.

Key terms
Deferred fixed overhead
Fixed MOH held in ending inventory under absorption costing.

Section 6.4Segmented income statements

A segment is any part of the business that managers want profit data for: a product line, a sales channel, a region, a store. Cobalt Trail sells through two channels, Online and Retail stores.

Traceable vs common fixed costs

A traceable fixed cost exists because of the segment: if the segment were dropped, the traceable fixed cost would disappear. The website's hosting and the online marketing team are traceable to Online; store leases are traceable to Retail.

A common fixed cost supports several segments at once and would stay even if one segment were dropped, such as the CEO's salary or the headquarters building. Common fixed costs should not be allocated to segments: any split would be arbitrary.

The segment margin is contribution margin minus traceable fixed costs. It's the best gauge of a segment's long-run profitability: Retail adds $80,000 to the company's profit.

Exam tip

Contribution margin is for short-run, volume decisions. Segment margin is for long-run decisions, such as keeping or dropping a segment.

Levels of segments

Segments can be broken down further. Online splits into the company website and an outside marketplace. The online team manager's salary is traceable to Online, but common to the website and the marketplace, because neither one alone would remove it. A cost can be traceable at one level and common at the level below.

Key terms
Traceable fixed cost
Exists because of the segment; disappears if the segment is dropped.
Common fixed cost
Supports several segments; stays if any one is dropped.
Segment margin
Segment CM − traceable fixed costs.

Section 6.5Segment break-even

A segment breaks even when its contribution margin covers its own traceable fixed costs:

Segment break-even sales = traceable fixed expenses ÷ segment CM ratio

Online: $180,000 ÷ 60% = $300,000. Retail: $120,000 ÷ 40% = $300,000.

The company as a whole must also cover the common fixed costs:

Company break-even sales = (traceable + common fixed expenses) ÷ overall CM ratio

Cobalt Trail: ($300,000 + $100,000) ÷ 50% = $800,000. That's more than the two segment break-evens added together ($600,000), because the common costs have to be covered too.

Exam tip

The company break-even assumes today's sales mix (here 50/50). If the mix changes, so does the overall CM ratio.

Section 6.6Common mistakes

Leaving costs out. Segment reports should include every cost the segment causes, all along the value chain: research, design, marketing, distribution and customer service, not just manufacturing.

Using a poor allocation base. When a cost is assigned to segments, the base should be what actually drives that cost. Sales dollars rarely drive a cost.

Splitting up common costs. Suppose Cobalt Trail spreads its $100,000 of common costs by floor space, and the stores use 90% of it. Retail is charged $90,000 and seems to lose $10,000 ($80,000 − $90,000). Dropping Retail looks smart, but the $100,000 of common costs would stay. The company would lose Retail's $80,000 segment margin, and NOI would fall from $100,000 to $20,000.

Exam tip

To decide whether to keep a segment, compare its segment margin with zero. Allocated common costs don't change the answer.

Key terms
Arbitrary allocation
Splitting a common cost among segments; it can make a profitable segment look unprofitable.
