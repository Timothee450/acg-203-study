Process Costing

Job-order costing tracks one order at a time. Many companies make the same product nonstop, so there are no separate jobs to track. This chapter costs that kind of output. The example is May at Seabright Hot Sauce Co., where every gallon of sauce passes through two departments: Blending, then Bottling.

Section 4.1Process vs job-order costing

Same goal, different unit

Both systems answer the same question: what does one unit cost? Both use the same accounts (Raw Materials, Work in Process, Finished Goods, Manufacturing Overhead, Cost of Goods Sold) and the same flow from materials to sales. The difference is where costs are collected.

Job-order costingProcess costing
OutputMany different jobsOne identical product, made continuously
Costs collected byJobDepartment
Key documentJob cost sheetDepartment production report
Unit costJob cost ÷ units in the jobDepartment cost ÷ equivalent units, for the period
ExamplesCustom signs, home builders, law casesHot sauce, paint, cereal, gasoline
Exam tip

If every unit is the same and production never stops, it's process costing. If each order is different, it's job-order costing.

Processing departments

A processing department is a stage of production that puts cost into the product: it uses materials, people, machines or all three. Every unit that passes through gets the same work, so what comes out is identical. Products usually move through departments in a fixed order.

At Seabright, chili mash and vinegar are cooked and blended in Blending. The sauce then moves to Bottling, which adds bottles, caps and labels.

In process costing, direct labor is often small next to materials and overhead, so labor and overhead are combined. Conversion cost is direct labor plus manufacturing overhead: the cost of turning materials into product.

Key terms
Process costing
Costing identical units made continuously, by department.
Processing department
A department that adds materials, labor or overhead, treating every unit the same way.
Conversion cost
Direct labor + manufacturing overhead.

Section 4.2Recording the flow

The entries look like job-order costing's, with one change: each department has its own Work in Process account, and costs are traced to departments instead of jobs.

Materials, labor and overhead

(1) Materials. Blending used $88,000 of chili mash, vinegar and spices. Bottling used $14,000 of bottles, caps and labels. Materials can be added in any department.

Work in Process–Blending88,000
Work in Process–Bottling14,000
Raw Materials102,000

(2) Labor. Direct labor is traced to departments: $18,900 in Blending and $12,000 in Bottling.

Work in Process–Blending18,900
Work in Process–Bottling12,000
Salaries and Wages Payable30,900

(3) Overhead. Each department applies overhead with its own predetermined rate. Blending applied $30 per machine-hour × 1,100 hours = $33,000; Bottling applied $40 per machine-hour × 500 hours = $20,000.

Work in Process–Blending33,000
Work in Process–Bottling20,000
Manufacturing Overhead53,000

So Blending's conversion cost for May is $18,900 + $33,000 = $51,900.

Transfers between departments

(4) Finished sauce leaves Blending for Bottling, carrying its cost with it: $136,000 (section 4.5 shows where this number comes from). In Bottling it is called transferred-in cost, and it's treated like one more input.

Work in Process–Bottling136,000
Work in Process–Blending136,000

(5) Bottling finished cases costing $180,000 and sent them to the warehouse. That is the cost of goods manufactured.

Finished Goods180,000
Work in Process–Bottling180,000

(6) Cases costing $170,000 were sold.

Cost of Goods Sold170,000
Finished Goods170,000

Bottling's T-account: $10,000 at May 1 + $136,000 transferred in + $14,000 materials + $12,000 labor + $20,000 overhead = $192,000. After $180,000 moves to Finished Goods, $12,000 is still in process.

Key terms
Transferred-in cost
Cost that arrives from an earlier department with the units.
Work in Process by department
Each processing department keeps its own Work in Process account.

Section 4.3Equivalent units

The idea

At the end of a month, a department almost always has units that are only partly done. You can't count a half-blended gallon as a whole gallon, and you can't ignore it either. Equivalent units convert partly finished units into the number of whole units the same work could have finished:

Equivalent units = partly finished units × percent complete

For example, 8,000 gallons that are 25% blended hold the same work as 2,000 finished gallons.

Materials and conversion are counted separately, because they're often added at different times. Spices might all go in at the start, while labor and overhead are spread across the whole process.

Weighted-average method

The weighted-average method blends last month's work on beginning inventory with this month's work. It counts every unit that left the department as complete, plus the work sitting in ending inventory:

Equivalent units = units completed and transferred out + equivalent units in ending work in process

Blending's units for May:

GallonsUnits
Beginning work in process (materials 75%, conversion 40% complete)4,000
Started in May36,000
Completed and transferred to Bottling34,000
Ending work in process (materials 80%, conversion 50% complete)6,000

Units to account for: 4,000 + 36,000 = 40,000. Units accounted for: 34,000 + 6,000 = 40,000. Now the equivalent units:

MaterialsConversion
Completed and transferred out34,00034,000
Ending work in process: 6,000 × 80% and 6,000 × 50%4,8003,000
Equivalent units of production38,80037,000

Materials: 34,000 + (6,000 × 80%) = 38,800. Conversion: 34,000 + (6,000 × 50%) = 37,000.

Exam tip

Under the weighted-average method, the percent complete of beginning work in process never enters the calculation. The 4,000 gallons that started May partly done are inside the 34,000 completed, counted as whole units.

The other method, FIFO, counts only the work done this period. It's covered in the textbook's appendix; this course's examples use weighted-average.

Section 4.4Cost per equivalent unit

With the weighted-average method, beginning inventory's cost is added to this month's cost, then divided by the equivalent units:

Cost per equivalent unit = (cost of beginning work in process + cost added this period) ÷ equivalent units

Blending, MayMaterialsConversion
Cost of beginning work in process$9,000$3,600
Cost added in May88,00051,900
Total cost$97,000$55,500
÷ Equivalent units38,80037,000
Cost per equivalent unit$2.50$1.50

Materials: $97,000 ÷ 38,800 = $2.50. Conversion: $55,500 ÷ 37,000 = $1.50. A fully blended gallon costs $2.50 + $1.50 = $4.00.

Exam tip

Divide each cost category by its own equivalent units. Materials cost never gets divided by conversion units.

Section 4.5Assigning costs and reconciling

Transferred out and ending inventory

Each department needs two numbers: the cost of the units it sent on, and the cost of the units still in its ending inventory.

Transferred out. Completed units are 100% done for every input: 34,000 × $4.00 = $136,000. That's the amount in entry (4).

Ending work in process. Use the equivalent units in ending inventory for each category:

Ending work in processEquivalent unitsCost per EUCost
Materials4,800$2.50$12,000
Conversion3,000$1.50$4,500
Total$16,500

So 4,800 × $2.50 = $12,000 and 3,000 × $1.50 = $4,500, for $16,500 still in Blending.

Cost reconciliation

A cost reconciliation report proves nothing was lost or double-counted: the costs the department had to account for must equal the costs it assigned.

If the two totals don't match, check the equivalent units first. The most common slip is using total units instead of equivalent units for ending inventory.

Section 4.6Operation costing

Some factories sit between the two systems. Operation costing is a hybrid used when batches of different products pass through the same operations.

Picture a fictional sneaker maker, Kestrel Footwear. It runs batches of leather boots and canvas sneakers through the same Cutting and Stitching departments. Materials differ a lot between batches, so they're traced to each batch, as in job-order costing. Cutting and stitching take about the same effort per pair, so conversion costs are averaged across all pairs in each operation, as in process costing. If leather costs $38 a pair, canvas $9 a pair, and cutting and stitching cost $4 and $6 a pair, a pair of boots costs $38 + $4 + $6 = $48 and a pair of sneakers costs $9 + $4 + $6 = $19.

CostTreated likeWhy
MaterialsJob-order: traced to each batchLeather costs far more than canvas
ConversionProcess: one rate per operationEvery pair gets the same cutting and stitching
Key terms
Operation costing
Hybrid system: materials by batch, conversion cost by operation.
