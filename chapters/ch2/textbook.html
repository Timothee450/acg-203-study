Job-Order Costing: Calculating Unit Product Costs

When every order is different, you have to cost each one separately. This chapter shows how: trace materials and labor to the job, then apply overhead with a rate set before the year starts. The examples follow Harborlight Sign Works, a fictional shop that builds custom signs to order.

Section 2.1Job-order costing

When it's used

A job-order costing system fits a business that makes many different products in a period, usually to a customer's order. Each order (a job) is unique, so its costs have to be tracked separately.

  • A shop building 20 storefront signs for one restaurant, then a single lobby sign for a dentist
  • A custom home builder, a ship repair yard, a print shop, a wedding caterer
  • Service firms too: a law firm costs each client's case; an ad agency costs each campaign

The opposite is a company that makes one identical product continuously, like a cereal maker or a paint factory. That calls for process costing, covered in Chapter 4.

The job cost sheet

Every job gets a job cost sheet, a record that collects all of the job's costs: direct materials, direct labor and applied overhead. Two source documents feed it:

  • Materials requisition form: a request to take materials out of the storeroom. It names the job the materials are for, so their cost is charged straight to that job as direct materials.
  • Employee time ticket: an hour-by-hour record of what a worker did. Hours spent on a job are charged to it as direct labor (hours × wage rate).

Direct materials and direct labor are traced. Manufacturing overhead can't be traced, because rent, utilities and supervisors serve every job at once, so it has to be allocated using an overhead rate.

Exam tip

Indirect materials and indirect labor never appear on a job cost sheet as such. They're part of manufacturing overhead and reach the job only through the overhead rate.

Key terms
Job-order costing
Costing each unique order (job) separately.
Job cost sheet
The record of a job's direct materials, direct labor and applied overhead.
Materials requisition form
Source document that charges direct materials to a specific job.
Employee time ticket
Source document that charges direct labor hours to a specific job.

Section 2.2Predetermined overhead rate

Allocation bases, and why the rate is set in advance

An allocation base is the measure used to spread overhead across jobs, such as direct labor-hours (DLH), direct labor cost or machine-hours (MH). A job that uses more of the base gets more overhead.

The rate is predetermined, meaning it's computed from estimates before the year begins. Why not wait and use actual figures?

  • Actual overhead isn't known until the year ends, but managers need job costs now, to price bids and value inventory.
  • Actual overhead jumps around month to month (heating in winter, repairs), which would make identical jobs cost different amounts depending on the month.
  • Activity varies by season. Spreading fixed overhead over a slow month's few hours would make jobs look expensive just because they were made in a slow month.

Computing the rate

Predetermined overhead rate = estimated total manufacturing overhead ÷ estimated total amount of the allocation base

The estimate is built in four steps:

  1. Estimate the total allocation base for next year. Harborlight expects 50,000 direct labor-hours.
  2. Estimate the total fixed overhead: $300,000 (rent, salaried supervisors, depreciation).
  3. Estimate the variable overhead per unit of the base: $2 per direct labor-hour (power, supplies).
  4. Total estimated overhead = fixed + variable rate × base: $300,000 + $2 × 50,000 = $400,000. Divide by the base.

$400,000 ÷ 50,000 DLH = $8 per direct labor-hour

Try it · Predetermined overhead rate
Estimated total overhead$400,000
Predetermined rate$8.00 / hour

Exam tip

Both halves of the rate are estimates. A question that gives you actual overhead and actual hours is setting a trap: the rate always uses the estimated figures.

Key terms
Allocation base
The measure (DLH, labor cost, MH) used to assign overhead to jobs.
Predetermined overhead rate
Estimated total overhead ÷ estimated total allocation base, set before the year.

Section 2.3Applying overhead

Rate × actual base

During the year, overhead is charged to each job as the job uses the base. This is called overhead application:

Overhead applied to a job = predetermined overhead rate × actual amount of the base used by the job

Harborlight's Job 214 is 20 storefront signs. Its time tickets show 120 direct labor-hours, so:

120 direct labor-hours × $8 = $960 of overhead applied

Notice the mix: the rate comes from estimates made last year, but the hours are the job's actual hours. Nobody waits for the year's actual overhead bill.

Exam tip

"Applied" overhead (rate × actual base) and "actual" overhead (the real bills) are different numbers. They're compared at year end in section 2.6.

Key terms
Overhead application
Charging overhead to jobs: predetermined rate × actual base used.
Applied overhead
The overhead charged to jobs during the period.

Section 2.4Total and unit job cost

Adding it up

The job cost sheet totals three lines:

Job 214 · 20 signsAmount
Direct materials (from requisitions)$2,600
Direct labor (120 hours × $20)$2,400
Manufacturing overhead applied (120 hours × $8)$960
Total job cost$5,960

The unit product cost is the total job cost divided by the units in the job: $5,960 ÷ 20 = $298 a sign. That is the cost each sign carries in inventory, and the starting point for pricing.

Try it · Job cost

Overhead applied$960
Total job cost$5,960
Unit product cost$298.00

Choosing a good base

Direct materials and direct labor are traced accurately. Overhead is only as accurate as the allocation base. The base should be a cost driver: something that actually causes overhead. In a shop full of automated cutting machines, machine-hours may drive overhead far better than labor-hours. Using a poor base gives some jobs too much overhead and others too little, which leads to bad prices and bad decisions.

Key terms
Total job cost
Direct materials + direct labor + applied overhead.
Unit product cost
Total job cost ÷ units in the job.
Cost driver
A factor that causes overhead costs; the best allocation base.

Section 2.5Multiple overhead rates

Departmental rates

A single plantwide overhead rate assumes one base drives all overhead. Many companies instead compute a separate rate for each department, each with the base that fits it. Harborlight has two departments:

Cutting (machine-heavy)Finishing (hand work)
Allocation baseMachine-hoursDirect labor-hours
Estimated base40,000 MH40,000 DLH
Estimated fixed overhead$180,000$60,000
Variable overhead per unit of base$3$1
Estimated total overhead$300,000$100,000
Departmental rate$7.50 per MH$2.50 per DLH

Job 214 used 80 machine-hours in Cutting and 90 direct labor-hours in Finishing (plus 30 labor-hours in Cutting, for 120 labor-hours in all):

  • Cutting: 80 machine-hours × $7.50 = $600
  • Finishing: 90 direct labor-hours × $2.50 = $225
  • Overhead applied: $825, not the $960 the plantwide rate gave

Job 214's cost becomes $2,600 + $2,400 + $825 = $5,825, or $291.25 a sign. The job is light on machine time, so the plantwide labor-hour rate was charging it overhead it didn't really cause.

Why it changes the price

Many job shops price by marking up cost. At a 50% markup, Job 214 would be bid at $5,960 × 1.5 = $8,940 using the plantwide rate, but $5,825 × 1.5 = $8,737.50 with departmental rates. With overcosted jobs, a shop loses bids it should win; with undercosted jobs, it wins bids that lose money.

Taking the idea further, a company can build a rate for each activity (setups, inspections, machine time). That is activity-based costing, the subject of Chapter 7.

Exam tip

With departmental rates, apply each department's rate to the job's base in that department, then add the departments together.

Key terms
Plantwide overhead rate
One predetermined rate for the whole factory.
Departmental overhead rates
A separate rate for each department, each with its own base.

Under- and overapplied overhead

Because the rate is built on estimates, the overhead applied to jobs during the year almost never equals the actual overhead incurred.

  • Underapplied overhead: applied is less than actual. The jobs were undercosted.
  • Overapplied overhead: applied is more than actual. The jobs were overcosted.

Harborlight actually used 49,000 direct labor-hours, so it applied 49,000 × $8 = $392,000. Its actual overhead bills came to $412,000. Overhead was underapplied by $20,000.

The simplest fix is to adjust cost of goods sold. Underapplied overhead is added to cost of goods sold, so it increases cost of goods sold and lowers net operating income. Overapplied overhead is subtracted from cost of goods sold, so it decreases cost of goods sold and raises net operating income. Chapter 3 covers the journal entries behind this.

Try it · Under or overapplied?
Overhead applied$392,000
Result$20,000 underapplied

Exam tip

Remember "under → COGS goes up". The jobs were charged too little, so cost of goods sold is topped up.

Service companies

Job-order costing isn't just for factories. A law firm treats each case as a job: lawyers' hours are direct labor, court filing fees are direct costs, and office overhead is applied with a rate per billable hour. Hospitals, architects, consultants and movie studios cost their work the same way.

Key terms
Subsidiary ledger
The set of job cost sheets behind work in process, finished goods and cost of goods sold.
Underapplied overhead
Applied < actual; added to cost of goods sold.
Overapplied overhead
Applied > actual; subtracted from cost of goods sold.
