Chapter 1 · Textbook

Managerial Accounting and Cost Concepts

Before you can plan, control or decide anything, you need to know what kind of cost you are looking at. This chapter gives you the labels. One fictional company, Northstar Kayak Co., runs through every example: it molds plastic kayaks in one factory and sells them to dealers and online.

Section 1.1Managerial accounting

Managerial vs financial accounting

Both kinds of accounting start from the same transactions. The difference is who reads the result.

  • Financial accounting reports to people outside the company: stockholders, lenders and regulators. It follows GAAP and looks back at what already happened.
  • Managerial accounting gives information to people inside the company (managers and employees) so they can plan, control operations and make decisions.
Financial accountingManagerial accounting
ReadersOutsiders: investors, creditors, regulatorsInsiders: managers and employees
Time focusThe pastThe future (budgets, plans)
PriorityPrecision and verifiabilityRelevance and timeliness
DetailThe company as a wholeSegments: products, departments, customers
RulesMust follow GAAPNo GAAP requirement; whatever helps the decision
Required?Yes, for public companiesNo, it is optional

The three jobs show up all through the course. Planning means setting goals and spelling out how to reach them, usually in a budget. Controlling means gathering feedback to make sure the plan is being followed, and changing course when it isn't. Decision making means choosing among options, such as whether to add a product or drop a customer.

Cost objects: direct and indirect costs

A cost object is anything you want to know the cost of: a product, a product line, a customer, a department. For Northstar, one cost object is a single Touring kayak.

  • Direct cost: a cost that can be easily and conveniently traced to the cost object. The resin poured into a Touring kayak's mold, and the wages of the crew who mold it, are direct.
  • Indirect cost: a cost that cannot be easily traced, usually because it is shared. Northstar's factory rent supports every kayak model at once, so it is indirect to any one kayak.

A shared cost like rent is called a common cost. Even if Northstar stopped making the Touring model, it would still pay the full rent.

Exam tip

"Direct" and "indirect" only make sense relative to a cost object. The plant manager's salary is indirect to a single kayak but direct to the factory as a whole. Always ask "direct to what?"

Key terms
Managerial accounting
Information for insiders to plan, control and decide.
Cost object
Anything you want to know the cost of.
Direct cost
Easily and conveniently traced to the cost object.
Indirect cost
Cannot be easily traced to the cost object; often a common cost shared by several objects.

Section 1.2Manufacturing costs

Direct materials, direct labor and manufacturing overhead

Every cost of making a product falls into one of three categories:

CategoryWhat it isNorthstar examples
Direct materials (DM)Materials that become an integral part of the finished product and can be easily traced to itPlastic resin, molded seats, hatch covers
Direct labor (DL)Wages of workers whose time can be easily traced to individual units (sometimes called "touch labor")Mold operators, the trim-and-assembly crew
Manufacturing overhead (MOH)Every other cost of making the product: the factory costs that can't be easily tracedFactory rent, factory electricity, the supervisor's salary, depreciation on molds, glue and sandpaper

Two things go into overhead that surprise students. Indirect materials such as glue and sandpaper are used up in making the product, but they cost so little per unit that tracing them isn't worth the effort. Indirect labor means people who work in the factory but don't touch individual units, such as supervisors, maintenance workers and security guards. Both are overhead.

Northstar's costs for one month, when it made 1,000 kayaks:

Manufacturing costAmount
Direct materials$60,000
Direct labor$40,000
Manufacturing overhead (rent $12,000, electricity $6,000, supervisors $15,000, mold depreciation $9,000, maintenance $5,000, indirect materials $3,000)$50,000
Total manufacturing cost$150,000
Cost per kayak ($150,000 ÷ 1,000)$150
Exam tip

If a factory cost isn't direct materials or direct labor, it is overhead. Overhead is defined by what it isn't.

Prime cost and conversion cost

Two subtotals combine the categories in overlapping pairs:

Prime cost = direct materials + direct labor

Conversion cost = direct labor + manufacturing overhead

Prime cost is everything you can trace directly to the product. Conversion cost is what it takes to convert raw materials into a finished product. Direct labor appears in both, so the two never add up to total manufacturing cost.

For Northstar: prime cost = $60,000 + $40,000 = $100,000. Conversion cost = $40,000 + $50,000 = $90,000. Together they make $190,000, which is $40,000 (the direct labor) more than the $150,000 total.

Nonmanufacturing costs: selling and administrative

Costs that have nothing to do with making the product fall into two groups:

  • Selling costs (also called marketing costs): what it takes to get orders and deliver products. Examples are advertising, sales commissions, shipping kayaks to customers and the salaries of the sales team.
  • Administrative costs: the general running of the company. Examples are the CEO's salary, the accounting and HR departments, and rent on the head office.
Exam tip

Location tells you a lot. Rent on the factory is overhead. Rent on the head office is administrative. Depreciation on factory equipment is overhead; depreciation on the sales team's cars is selling.

Key terms
Direct materials
Materials that become part of the product and are easily traced to it.
Direct labor
Wages for work easily traced to individual units.
Manufacturing overhead
All manufacturing costs except direct materials and direct labor.
Prime cost
Direct materials + direct labor.
Conversion cost
Direct labor + manufacturing overhead.
Selling and administrative costs
Nonmanufacturing costs of getting orders, delivering products and running the company.

Section 1.3Product and period costs

The two groups

For financial statements, every cost lands in one of two groups. The question is when it becomes an expense.

Product costs

All the costs of making the product: direct materials, direct labor and manufacturing overhead. They are also called inventoriable costs because they are first recorded as inventory, an asset on the balance sheet. They become an expense, cost of goods sold, only when the product is sold.

Period costs

All selling and administrative costs. They are expensed on the income statement in the period they are incurred, whether or not anything was sold. They never sit in inventory.

Product costs follow a simple matching idea: the cost of making something should hit the income statement in the same period as the revenue from selling it.

From inventory to expense

In a manufacturer, product costs pass through inventory accounts on the balance sheet before reaching the income statement. Only direct materials start in Raw materials. When they go into production they move to Work in process, and that is where direct labor and manufacturing overhead are added. Finished units move to Finished goods, and become cost of goods sold when they are sold:

Raw materials (DM) → Work in process (+ DL + MOH) → Finished goods → Cost of goods sold (when sold)

Northstar made 1,000 kayaks at $150 each, for $150,000 of product cost. It sold 800 this month:

Where the $150,000 goesKayaksAmountStatement
Cost of goods sold (expense)800$120,000Income statement, this month
Finished goods inventory (asset)200$30,000Balance sheet, until sold

Northstar's selling costs of $18,000 and administrative costs of $22,000 are period costs. The full $40,000 is expensed this month, even though 200 kayaks haven't sold yet.

Try it · Where product costs end up
Cost of goods sold$120,000
Finished goods inventory$30,000

Exam tip

Factory costs go to inventory first, even the factory's rent and electricity. Only selling and administrative costs skip straight to expense.

Key terms
Product (inventoriable) cost
DM + DL + MOH; held in inventory until the product is sold, then expensed as cost of goods sold.
Period cost
Selling and administrative costs; expensed in the period incurred.
Cost of goods sold
The product cost of the units sold this period.

Section 1.4Cost behavior

Cost behavior means how a cost reacts when the level of activity changes. The measure of activity is the activity base, also called the cost driver: units produced, units sold, machine hours, miles driven and so on. Northstar's main activity base is kayaks produced.

Variable costs

A variable cost changes in total in direct proportion to activity, but stays constant per unit. Northstar's resin costs $45 a kayak:

Kayaks madeResin per kayakTotal resin cost
500$45$22,500
1,000$45$45,000
1,500$45$67,500

Direct materials, direct labor paid by the unit, sales commissions and shipping costs are typical variable costs.

Fixed costs

A fixed cost stays the same in total no matter how much activity there is, so it gets smaller per unit as activity rises. Northstar's factory rent is $12,000 a month:

Kayaks madeTotal rentRent per kayak
500$12,000$24
1,000$12,000$12
1,500$12,000$8

Because the per-unit figure keeps changing, it is misleading. Managers should think about fixed costs in total.

Fixed costs come in two kinds:

  • Committed fixed costs come from long-term investments in buildings, equipment and the basic organization: depreciation, rent on a lease, insurance, key managers' salaries. They can't be cut quickly without hurting the business.
  • Discretionary fixed costs (also called managed fixed costs) come from yearly management decisions: advertising, research, training, internships. They can be cut for a while without lasting damage.
When activity rises…In totalPer unit
Variable costRisesStays the same
Fixed costStays the sameFalls
Exam tip

The classic trap is to multiply a per-unit fixed cost by a new volume. Fixed cost per unit is only valid at the volume it was calculated for.

The relevant range

"Fixed" only holds within a band of activity called the relevant range. Northstar's building can turn out at most 1,500 kayaks a month. To make more, it would need a second building, and rent would jump to $20,000. So the $12,000 rent is fixed only from 0 to 1,500 kayaks. Graphed, many fixed costs look like a staircase: flat inside each range, then a step up.

Variable costs have a relevant range too. Outside it, for example with a bulk discount on resin, the cost per unit may change.

Mixed costs

A mixed cost (also called a semivariable cost) has both a fixed part and a variable part. Northstar's factory electricity has a base charge of $2,000 a month, plus about $4 for every kayak molded. A mixed cost is written as a straight line:

Y = a + bX

  • Y = total mixed cost
  • a = total fixed cost (where the line crosses the vertical axis)
  • b = variable cost per unit of activity (the slope)
  • X = level of activity

At 1,000 kayaks: Y = $2,000 + $4 × 1,000 = $6,000. That is the $6,000 of electricity in Northstar's overhead.

Section 1.5Costs for decisions

Every decision compares options. Only costs and benefits that differ between the options are relevant. Everything else can be ignored. Northstar's example: it has 200 leftover kayaks of an older model and can sell them two ways.

Sell to a dealerSell onlineDifference
Revenue$40,000$46,000+$6,000
Shipping$3,000$5,000+$2,000
Website and payment fees$0$2,000+$2,000
Warehouse rent given up (opportunity cost)$0$1,500+$1,500
Molds bought last year for this model (sunk)$8,000$8,000$0
Advantage of selling online$500

Differential cost and revenue

A differential cost is the difference in cost between two options. A differential revenue is the difference in revenue. A cost increase is also called an incremental cost, and a decrease a decremental cost. Here selling online brings $6,000 of differential revenue and $4,000 of differential cost (shipping $2,000 + fees $2,000).

Differential costs can be fixed or variable. All that matters is that they differ between the options.

Opportunity cost

An opportunity cost is the benefit you give up by choosing one option over another. It is never recorded in the accounting records, but it belongs in the decision. Selling online means storing the kayaks in a corner of the warehouse that Northstar could otherwise rent to a neighbor for $1,500. With it, the online option wins by only $500 ($6,000 − $4,000 − $1,500).

Another example: a student who takes an unpaid internship gives up the $3,000 they could have earned at a summer job. That $3,000 is the internship's opportunity cost.

Sunk cost

A sunk cost has already been incurred and cannot be changed by any decision now or in the future. The $8,000 Northstar spent last year on molds for the older model is gone either way, so it appears in both columns and cancels out.

Exam tip

Sunk costs are never relevant. Future costs that are the same under every option aren't relevant either. Relevant = future and different.

Key terms
Differential cost / revenue
The difference in cost or revenue between two options.
Opportunity cost
The benefit given up by choosing one option over another; not in the accounting records.
Sunk cost
Already incurred and unchangeable; never relevant.

Section 1.6Income statement formats

Traditional vs contribution format

The same numbers can be arranged two ways. The example here is a merchandising company, Harbor Outfitters, a shop that buys kayak paddles and resells them. It sold 1,000 paddles at $100 each. Each paddle cost $60 to buy, the selling costs were $5 a paddle plus $10,000 fixed, and the administrative costs were $2 a paddle plus $13,000 fixed.

Traditional format

Sorts costs by function. Used for external reports.

Sales$100,000
Cost of goods sold(60,000)
Gross margin40,000
Selling expenses(15,000)
Administrative expenses(15,000)
Net operating income$10,000

Contribution format

Sorts costs by behavior. Used inside the company.

Sales$100,000
Variable expenses (60,000 + 5,000 + 2,000)(67,000)
Contribution margin33,000
Fixed expenses (10,000 + 13,000)(23,000)
Net operating income$10,000

Gross margin = sales − cost of goods sold. Contribution margin = sales − all variable expenses, including variable selling and administrative costs: $100,000 − $67,000 = $33,000. Contribution margin is what is left to cover fixed expenses and then provide profit. The net operating income is the same $10,000 either way, because only the arrangement changes.

Exam tip

In a merchandising company, cost of goods sold is a variable cost, so it belongs with the variable expenses in the contribution format. Don't forget the variable selling and administrative costs.

Why managers use the contribution format

Because it separates variable from fixed costs, the contribution format shows instantly how profit changes with volume: each extra paddle adds its contribution margin of $33 ($100 − $67) to profit, while fixed costs stay put. Later chapters build on it:

  • Cost-volume-profit analysis (Chapter 5)
  • Segment reporting (Chapter 6)
  • Budgeting (Chapter 8)
  • Special decisions such as pricing and make-or-buy (Chapter 13)
Key terms
Traditional format
Costs sorted by function; shows gross margin.
Contribution format
Costs sorted by behavior; shows contribution margin.
Contribution margin
Sales − variable expenses; what is left to cover fixed expenses and provide profit.
