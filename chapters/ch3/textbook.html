Chapter 3 · Textbook

Job-Order Costing: Cost Flows and External Reporting

Chapter 2 costed one job at a time. This chapter follows the dollars through the accounting records for a whole month: journal entries, T-accounts, the schedules of cost of goods manufactured and cost of goods sold, and what to do with leftover overhead. The example is March at Harborlight Sign Works, which applies overhead at $8 per direct labor-hour (from Chapter 2).

Section 3.1How costs flow

The accounts

  • Raw Materials (asset): materials bought but not yet put into production, both direct and indirect.
  • Work in Process (asset): jobs started but not finished. Its detail is the job cost sheets of unfinished jobs.
  • Finished Goods (asset): jobs completed but not yet sold.
  • Cost of Goods Sold (expense): the cost of jobs sold this period.
  • Manufacturing Overhead: a temporary clearing account. Actual overhead costs are debited to it; overhead applied to jobs is credited out of it.

The big picture

Raw Materials → Work in Process → Finished Goods → Cost of Goods Sold

Direct materials and direct labor go straight into Work in Process. Indirect materials, indirect labor and all other factory costs go into Manufacturing Overhead first, then reach Work in Process through the predetermined rate. Selling and administrative costs never enter this chain; they're expensed right away.

Section 3.2Recording the flow

Each March transaction below shows the journal entry. Debits are listed first; credits are indented.

Materials

(1) Purchase. Harborlight bought $50,000 of raw materials on account.

Raw Materials50,000
Accounts Payable50,000

(2) Issue. Requisitions took $46,000 of materials out of the storeroom: $40,000 of direct materials for specific jobs and $6,000 of indirect materials (glue, fasteners). Direct materials are debited to Work in Process; indirect materials are debited to Manufacturing Overhead.

Work in Process40,000
Manufacturing Overhead6,000
Raw Materials46,000

Labor

(3) March factory payroll was $92,000. Time tickets show $80,000 of direct labor (4,000 hours × $20). The other $12,000 is indirect labor: supervisors and maintenance. Direct labor goes to Work in Process; indirect labor is charged to Manufacturing Overhead.

Work in Process80,000
Manufacturing Overhead12,000
Salaries and Wages Payable92,000

Overhead: actual in, applied out

(4) Other actual overhead. Factory utilities $4,000, depreciation on factory equipment $10,000 and expired factory insurance $2,000, so $16,000 in all:

Manufacturing Overhead16,000
Accounts Payable4,000
Accumulated Depreciation10,000
Prepaid Insurance2,000

Actual overhead for March is now $6,000 + $12,000 + $16,000 = $34,000, all on the debit side of Manufacturing Overhead.

(5) Applied overhead. Jobs are charged at the predetermined rate, never with the actual bills: 4,000 hours × $8 = $32,000.

Work in Process32,000
Manufacturing Overhead32,000
Exam tip

Actual overhead costs are never debited to Work in Process. They go to Manufacturing Overhead. Only applied overhead moves into Work in Process.

Selling and administrative costs

(6) Sales salaries $15,000, advertising $5,000 and depreciation on office equipment $2,000, so $22,000. These are period costs: they're debited to expense accounts and never touch Work in Process or Manufacturing Overhead.

Finished and sold

(7) Jobs costing $150,000 were completed and moved to the finished goods warehouse:

Finished Goods150,000
Work in Process150,000

(8) Jobs that cost $140,000 were sold on account for $210,000. Two entries: one for the sale, one for the cost.

Accounts Receivable210,000
Sales210,000
Cost of Goods Sold140,000
Finished Goods140,000

In T-account form, Work in Process starts at $20,000, takes in $40,000 + $80,000 + $32,000, and sends out $150,000, leaving $22,000. Finished Goods: $30,000 + $150,000 − $140,000 = $40,000.

Section 3.4Under- and overapplied overhead

Finding the balance

At the end of the period, Manufacturing Overhead almost always has a balance left over:

Manufacturing OverheadDebit (actual)Credit (applied)
Indirect materials6,000
Indirect labor12,000
Other factory costs16,000
Applied to jobs32,000
Totals$34,000$32,000

A debit balance means actual exceeded applied: overhead is underapplied (here by $2,000). A credit balance means applied exceeded actual: overhead is overapplied. The applied figure came from 4,000 hours × $8 = $32,000.

Exam tip

The balance is the gap between actual and applied, not between estimated and actual. Estimates only matter for setting the rate.

Method 1: close to cost of goods sold

The simple method moves the whole balance into Cost of Goods Sold. Underapplied overhead increases cost of goods sold; overapplied overhead decreases it.

Cost of Goods Sold2,000
Manufacturing Overhead2,000

For an overapplied balance, the entry flips: debit Manufacturing Overhead, credit Cost of Goods Sold.

Method 2: allocate among the accounts

The more accurate method spreads the balance over Work in Process, Finished Goods and Cost of Goods Sold, in proportion to the overhead applied that sits in each account's ending balance. Of March's $32,000 applied, 15% is still in ending Work in Process, 25% in ending Finished Goods and 60% went to jobs that were sold:

AccountShare of applied overheadShare of $2,000
Work in Process15%$300
Finished Goods25%$500
Cost of Goods Sold60%$1,200

Exam tip

Most companies use method 1 when the balance is small. Allocating is more accurate when the balance is large, because inventory was also mis-costed.

Key terms
Underapplied overhead
Debit balance in Manufacturing Overhead: actual > applied.
Overapplied overhead
Credit balance in Manufacturing Overhead: applied > actual.
Allocation method
Spreading the balance over WIP, FG and COGS in proportion to applied overhead in each.
