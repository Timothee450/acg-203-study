Cost-Volume-Profit Relationships

Cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis shows how profit moves when prices, costs and sales volume change. It answers questions like "how many do we have to sell to stop losing money?" The example is a month at Brightwick Candle Co., a small maker of hand-poured candles.

Section 5.1Contribution margin

The assumptions

CVP keeps the math simple by assuming a few things hold within the relevant range:

  • The selling price per unit stays constant.
  • Costs are linear: variable cost is the same per unit, and fixed cost is the same in total.
  • In a company with several products, the sales mix stays constant.
  • In a manufacturing company, inventories don't change: everything made is sold.

Contribution margin per unit

Brightwick sells each candle for $25. Wax, wick, jar and a base sales commission cost $15 per candle. Rent, salaries and equipment cost $40,000 a month no matter how many candles sell.

Unit contribution margin = selling price − variable expense per unit = $25 − $15 = $10

Each candle sold contributes $10, first toward covering the fixed expenses and then, once those are covered, to profit. In a month when Brightwick sells 5,000 candles, sales are $125,000 and variable expenses are $75,000:

Contribution formatTotalPer candle
Sales (5,000 candles)$125,000$25
Variable expenses(75,000)(15)
Contribution margin$50,000$10
Fixed expenses(40,000)
Net operating income$10,000

Sell one more candle and net operating income rises by exactly $10, because fixed expenses don't move.

CM ratio and variable expense ratio

The contribution margin ratio is the share of each sales dollar left after variable expenses: $10 ÷ $25 = 40%. The variable expense ratio is the rest: $15 ÷ $25 = 60%. Together they always add to 100%.

The CM ratio turns a change in sales dollars into a change in profit. If Brightwick's sales rise by $20,000, contribution margin rises by $20,000 × 40% = $8,000, and with fixed expenses unchanged, so does net operating income.

Exam tip

Use unit CM when the question is in units, and the CM ratio when it's in sales dollars.

The profit equation

Profit = (Sales − Variable expenses) − Fixed expenses

Written with units (Q) or with sales dollars:

Profit = Unit CM × Q − Fixed expenses = $10 × 5,000 − $40,000 = $10,000

Profit = CM ratio × Sales − Fixed expenses = 40% × $125,000 − $40,000 = $10,000

Contribution margin
Sales − variable expenses; what's left to cover fixed expenses and then provide profit.
CM ratio
Contribution margin ÷ sales (or unit CM ÷ price).
Variable expense ratio
Variable expenses ÷ sales; 1 − CM ratio.

Section 5.2Operating leverage

Operating leverage measures how sensitive net operating income is to a change in sales. A company with high fixed costs and low variable costs has high operating leverage: small changes in sales cause big swings in profit.

Degree of operating leverage = contribution margin ÷ net operating income

At 5,000 candles, Brightwick's degree of operating leverage is $50,000 ÷ $10,000 = 5. So a 10% rise in sales lifts net operating income by 5 × 10% = 50%, from $10,000 to $15,000.

5,000 candles5,500 candles (+10%)
Contribution margin ($10 each)$50,000$55,000
Fixed expenses(40,000)(40,000)

Exam tip

The degree of operating leverage is not constant. It's highest just above the break-even point and falls as sales grow, because net operating income grows faster than contribution margin.

Key terms
Degree of operating leverage
CM ÷ NOI at a given sales level; % change in NOI = DOL × % change in sales.

Section 5.3Break-even and margin of safety

Break-even point

The break-even point is the sales level where profit is exactly zero: total contribution margin equals fixed expenses.

Equation method. Set profit to zero and solve: $0 = $10 × Q − $40,000, so Q = 4,000 candles.

Formula method.

Break-even units = fixed expenses ÷ unit CM = $40,000 ÷ $10 = 4,000 candles

Break-even sales = fixed expenses ÷ CM ratio = $40,000 ÷ 40% = $100,000

Check: 4,000 candles × $25 = $100,000.

Margin of safety

The margin of safety is how far sales can fall before the company starts losing money.

Margin of safety = current sales − break-even sales = $125,000 − $100,000 = $25,000

As a percentage of sales: $25,000 ÷ $125,000 = 20%. In units: 5,000 − 4,000 = 1,000 candles.

Exam tip

The margin of safety percentage is divided by current sales, not by break-even sales. Here it's also the reciprocal of operating leverage: 1 ÷ 5 = 20%.

Key terms
Break-even point
Sales where NOI = 0: fixed expenses ÷ unit CM (units) or ÷ CM ratio (dollars).
Margin of safety
Current sales − break-even sales; as a % of current sales.

Section 5.4Target profit

Target profit analysis finds the sales needed to earn a chosen profit. Treat the target like one more fixed cost that contribution margin must cover:

Units to reach target = (fixed expenses + target profit) ÷ unit CM

Sales to reach target = (fixed expenses + target profit) ÷ CM ratio

To earn $20,000 a month, Brightwick needs ($40,000 + $20,000) ÷ $10 = 6,000 candles, or ($40,000 + $20,000) ÷ 40% = $150,000 of sales.

Section 5.5What-if changes

Testing a change

Managers use CVP to test a decision before making it. The shortcut is incremental analysis: look only at what changes. Start from 5,000 candles and $10,000 of profit.

(a) Advertise. Spending $6,000 more on ads is expected to raise sales by $20,000. Contribution margin rises $20,000 × 40% = $8,000; fixed expenses rise $6,000. Net operating income goes up $2,000, so the ads pay off.

(b) Cut the price. Dropping the price $2 to $23 is expected to raise volume 30%, to 6,500 candles. The new unit CM is $8, so contribution margin is 6,500 × $8 = $52,000 and net operating income is $52,000 − $40,000 = $12,000: up $2,000.

(c) Pay commissions instead of salary. Replacing $5,000 of fixed sales salaries with an extra $1-per-candle commission is expected to raise volume 10%, to 5,500 candles. Unit CM falls to $9; contribution margin is 5,500 × $9 = $49,500; fixed expenses fall to $35,000. Net operating income is $14,500: up $4,500.

Exam tip

Only list what changes: the change in contribution margin minus the change in fixed costs equals the change in net operating income.

Sales commissions

Paying salespeople a percentage of sales can backfire: they push whatever has the highest price, even when it earns the least. Paying commissions on contribution margin lines up what the salesperson wants with what the company wants.

ProductPriceVariable expense (before commission)Unit CM
Gift set$60$48$12
Large jar candle$40$22$18

A commission on sales rewards the $60 gift set, yet the $40 jar adds $6 more to profit per sale.

Key terms
Incremental analysis
Comparing only the revenues and costs that change.

Section 5.6The CVP graph

A CVP graph puts units sold on the horizontal axis and dollars on the vertical axis. It's built in four steps:

  1. Draw a flat fixed expense line at $40,000.
  2. Draw the total expense line: it starts at $40,000 with zero units and rises $15 for each candle.
  3. Draw the sales line: it starts at $0 and rises $25 for each candle.
  4. Where sales cross total expenses is the break-even point: 4,000 candles and $100,000.

Left of the crossing, the gap between the lines is the loss; to the right, it's the profit. At 5,000 candles the gap is $125,000 − $115,000 = $10,000.

A simpler profit graph plots profit directly: a single line starting at −$40,000 with zero sales and rising $10 per candle, crossing zero at 4,000.

Key terms
CVP graph
Sales and total expense lines; they cross at the break-even point.
Profit graph
Profit vs units: starts at −fixed expenses, slope = unit CM.

Section 5.7Sales mix

Sales mix is the relative share of each product in total sales. When products have different CM ratios, break-even depends on the mix.

Suppose Brightwick also sells reed diffusers, with fixed expenses for both lines of $72,000 a month:

CandlesDiffusersTotal
Sales$120,000 (60%)$80,000 (40%)$200,000
CM ratio40%60%
Contribution margin$48,000$48,000$96,000

The overall CM ratio is $96,000 ÷ $200,000 = 48%, so break-even sales are $72,000 ÷ 48% = $150,000.

Now suppose the mix shifts toward candles: $150,000 of candles and $50,000 of diffusers. Contribution margin falls to $60,000 + $30,000 = $90,000, the overall CM ratio to 45%, and break-even rises to $72,000 ÷ 45% = $160,000, even though total sales didn't change.

Exam tip

A shift toward the lower-CM-ratio product raises the break-even point and lowers profit. A shift toward the higher-CM-ratio product does the opposite.
